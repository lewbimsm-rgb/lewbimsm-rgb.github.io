```quick
When to speed up in pickleball: when the ball is above the net, you are balanced, and your opponent's paddle is low or out of position. Otherwise keep dinking. Good players attack less than you think. They just pick better moments.
```

I speed up too often. Most of us do. A ball sits up a little, something in the brain says "now," and the next thing you know it is in the net or sailing past the baseline. This post is the checklist I use to slow that voice down. It is for rec players who would rather win the point in three calm shots than lose it in one exciting one.

## When to speed up in pickleball: the three green lights

1. **The ball is above the net when you hit it.** If you have to lift it over, it is not an attack ball.
2. **You are balanced.** Weight forward, feet set, not reaching or leaning.
3. **Your opponent is in trouble.** Paddle down by the hip, moving sideways, or standing a step behind the kitchen line.

Three lights on: go. Two: maybe, if you are feeling good. One: dink it back and wait for a better one.

## The two red flags

- **The ball is below the net.** You will have to swing upward, which pops it up, and now they have the easy attack.
- **You are stretched or off balance.** Even if the shot lands, you will not be ready for the next ball, and there is almost always a next ball.

```compare
Situation | Attack or dink? | Why
High ball, you are set, their paddle is low | Attack | All three lights are on
High ball, but you are reaching | Dink | You will not be ready for the reply
Ball below the net, they look lost | Dink, a little deeper | Lifting it hands them the attack
Low ball, you are set, they are ready | Dink | Patience wins this one
```

## Aim at a place, not a person

The safest target is the hip of their paddle hand. It is awkward to defend, and if they do get it back, it usually comes back soft. The shoulder on the same side is the second choice. Aiming at the open court feels bolder, but it gives them time and room.

## Then expect it to come back

A speed-up rarely ends the point on its own. Keep the swing short and compact, finish with the paddle out in front of your chest, and be ready to block the reply softly back to their feet. If you find yourself swinging so hard that your paddle ends up behind you, you have traded the point for a highlight.

## The ten-minute drill

Play kitchen-line games with one rule: you may only attack a ball you take above the net with both feet set, and you have to say "green" out loud as you hit it. Attack without saying it and the point goes to the other side. It feels silly for about two minutes. Then you start seeing the green lights before you swing, which is the whole idea.

Fewer hands battles also means fewer wild swings, and at our age that is also fewer sore shoulders the next morning. Calm is a strategy, not a compromise.

> **SmartSelect Tip:** If you cannot say in one sentence why you attacked that ball, you probably should have dinked it.

```faq
Q: Should beginners ever speed up?
A: Yes, when all three green lights are on. Learn to dink reliably first, then add the attack as a choice rather than a reflex.
Q: Where should I aim a speed-up?
A: At the hip of your opponent's paddle hand, or the shoulder on that side. Those spots are hard to defend and tend to come back soft.
Q: What if the other team keeps speeding up on me?
A: Keep your paddle up in front of your chest, stay relaxed, and block the ball softly back to their feet. Most speed-ups lose to a calm block.
```
