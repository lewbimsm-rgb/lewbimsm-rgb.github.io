## Choose the ball before the shot

An attacking opportunity depends on more than a ball being close to the net. Consider contact height, your balance, the opponent’s paddle position, and the angle available. A ball below net height may require lifting, which increases risk. Advanced play often means declining a tempting attack until the conditions are better, not producing more spectacular winners.

## Plan for the counter

A speed-up does not end the rally automatically. Expect the opponent to block or counter and recover the paddle immediately. Avoid a large follow-through that leaves your body open. If you attack while leaning outside your base, your next contact may be weak even if the first shot stays in. Keep attacks controlled enough to support the next exchange.

## Use constrained points

Play kitchen-line points where attacks are allowed only from a balanced position. After each attack, name the cue that made it sensible: a high ball, a displaced opponent, or a clear lane. If no cue existed, replay the rally softly. This exercise builds decision quality without requiring every rally to turn into a hands battle.

## Review patterns, not highlights

With permission, record a practice game and note what happens in the two shots after each speed-up. Count immediate errors and weak recoveries alongside winners. A small sample can suggest a habit, but it is not a reliable overall performance rating. Professional players benefit from individual coaching and opponent-specific analysis; this guide is a practice framework rather than a substitute for that work.
