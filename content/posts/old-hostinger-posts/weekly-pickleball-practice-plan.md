## Pick one priority

A useful training week begins with one specific goal: deeper returns, more consistent drops, or clearer partner communication. “Get better” is too broad to guide a session. Choose something you can observe and repeat. Your available time, fitness, and experience should shape the schedule; more sessions are not automatically more effective.

## Separate learning from competing

Use one session for cooperative drills, one for constrained points, and one for ordinary games if your schedule allows. A drill isolates the skill, a constrained game introduces decisions, and match play tests transfer. For a return-depth goal, feed returns first, then play points where the return is tracked, then observe it during a normal game.

## Leave room to recover

Schedule breaks and adjust when soreness, fatigue, or heat affects your movement. Easy walking or gentle mobility can fit a recovery day, but do not turn every day into another hard workout. General conditioning can support court play; individualized strength and return-from-injury plans belong with qualified professionals. Stop activity that produces sharp pain.

## Review in five minutes

Write down the skill practiced, one useful cue, and what happened during games. A simple count from a controlled drill can show trends, but do not compare yourself to someone practicing under different conditions. At the end of the week, keep the priority if it still needs work or choose the next bottleneck. Progress comes from repeatable attention, not constantly changing equipment or collecting dozens of disconnected tips.
