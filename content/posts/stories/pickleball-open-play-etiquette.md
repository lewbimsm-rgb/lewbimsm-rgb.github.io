Walk up to open play for the first time and you will see a row of paddles leaning against the fence and eight people who all seem to know what it means. Nobody explains it. Put your paddle on the bench instead and you can wait a long while for a turn that is never coming, because the bench is not the line. Most pickleball open play etiquette is like that: small, friendly, and written down nowhere.

```quick
**The short version:** put your paddle in the line and ask how the rotation works at that court. Call the score out loud before you serve. Call the lines on your own side, and give the other team the close ones. Stop the moment a ball rolls onto a court. The rest is being pleasant.
```

## How the paddle line works

The paddles are the queue. Whether they sit in a rack, hang in a wooden holder or lean against the fence, the order they are in is the order people play. When a game ends, the next four paddles go on.

What happens to the four who just played depends on the court, which is why the most useful sentence in pickleball is "How does the rotation work here?" At some parks all four come off and go to the back of the line. At others the winners stay, split up, and play one more. Games usually go to eleven, win by two. The regulars would much rather explain it than untangle it afterward, so ask before you put your paddle down.

## Pickleball open play etiquette on the court

Call the score before every serve, loud enough for the far baseline, and wait until the other side is looking at you. It is three numbers: your score, their score, and whether you are the first or second server. For my first month I said it like a question. Nobody minded.

Lines work on the honor system. You call the balls on your side and they call the balls on theirs, and a ball that touches any part of the line is in. If you did not see it clearly, it was in, because the point is not worth the doubt. The [official rules](https://usapickleball.org/rules/) say the same thing in more words.

When a ball from another court rolls onto yours, anybody who sees it shouts "ball on!" and everybody stops, even in the middle of a good rally. You replay the point. It feels fussy until you watch someone step backward onto a ball, and then it feels like the best rule in the game.

Two smaller ones. Wait for a point to finish before you walk behind somebody's court. And when the game is over, meet at the net and tap paddles, whether you won eleven to two or lost eleven to two.

## When you are the newest one there

Say so. "I'm pretty new" before the first serve is all it takes, and most people will slow the ball down and explain as they go, since every one of them remembers their own first morning. One sorry a game is plenty. After that, your partner would rather you laughed.

If one court is plainly faster than you are ready for, look for a sign with level numbers on it, or ask which courts the newer players use. Nobody will be offended. And hold back on advice for other people unless they ask, even when you are right.

I have only played at a handful of parks, so when the regulars at yours do something differently, their way wins.

Bring a spare ball, learn two names, and thank whoever explains the paddle line. By your third visit somebody new will be asking you.
