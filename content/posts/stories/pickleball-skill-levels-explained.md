A man at the sign-in table once asked me, "What level are you?" and I said, "Tuesday." I thought he wanted to know which session I had come for. He was very nice about it. If the numbers on the court schedule mean as little to you as they did to me, here are pickleball skill levels explained the way I wish someone had done it that morning.

```quick
**The short version:** levels run from 1.0 up to about 5.5. Still learning the rules is a 2.0. Keeping a short rally going is a 2.5. Serves and returns that usually go in, plus the beginnings of a dink, make a 3.0. Most of us at open play live somewhere between 2.5 and 3.5, and stay there happily for years.
```

## What the numbers look like on a court

These are my plain-word versions of the definitions [USA Pickleball](https://usapickleball.org/) publishes. The official ones run a page per level.

```compare
Level | What it looks like
2.0 | Brand new. Learning the rules and how to keep score.
2.5 | Can keep a short rally going with people at the same level. Knows the basic rules, though the score still takes a second.
3.0 | Serves and returns usually land in, at a medium pace. Knows where to stand. Starting to dink, without much control yet.
3.5 | Sends the ball where she means to. Can hold a dink rally, gets up to the kitchen line quickly, and is working on a soft third shot.
4.0 | Steady. Gives away few free points, changes speeds on purpose, and plays with a plan.
```

## Three checks that place you in five minutes

You do not need a coach for this, only a friend and a bucket of balls.

First, serve ten. If eight land in the box, your serve is fine for a 3.0 game, because at that level nobody needs it to be clever, only to start the point.

Second, keep score for a whole game without help, including which server you are. It sounds small. It is the thing that tells a group you have played before.

Third, stand at the kitchen line with your friend across the net and try to dink three in a row each, softly, into the kitchen. If you can, you have the beginnings of the shot that separates a 3.0 from a 2.5.

Three for three, and you can write 3.0 on the sign-up sheet without anyone raising an eyebrow. Two of three, call it 2.5 and enjoy the company, since that is where I had the most fun.

## Where the official numbers come from

At a park, the number is whatever you say it is, on your honor. Clubs and tournaments use computer ratings that move up and down with your match results. You will hear two names: DUPR, which many clubs use, and UTR-P, which USA Pickleball adopted for its own tournaments. If you only play open play, you can ignore both for as long as you like. I have never entered a tournament, so the fine print of those ratings is beyond me, and their own websites explain it better than I could.

## Pickleball skill levels explained in one sentence

They exist so that games are fair and fun, and for no other reason. Sign up for the session that matches where you are now. Playing with a stronger group once in a while is how most people improve, but ask first, because a kind "of course, come on" is a better start than turning up and hoping.

Most of the women I play with have been a 3.0 for years. Their knees have opinions, their dinks are lovely, and none of them is in any hurry to become a 3.5. The level that matters is "fun to play with": calls the score, gives the close ones on the lines, laughs at her own bad shot.

When in doubt, write down the lower number and let somebody else tell you that you are better than that. It is a much nicer way to find out.
