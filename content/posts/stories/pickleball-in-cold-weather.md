The first cold morning of the season, I hit a perfectly ordinary serve and the ball split in half. One piece went over the net and the other one did not. We stood there deciding whose point it was, and then somebody said, "It's forty-four degrees," as though that explained everything. It did. Pickleball in cold weather is the same game played with slightly different equipment, and nobody mentions it until the first ball breaks.

```quick
**The short version:** below about 50 degrees the ball gets hard, bounces lower and cracks more often, so bring spares and bend your knees. Dress in thin layers you can take off. Keep your hands warm between games. Warm up for longer than you think you need to, and stay off any court that is wet or frosty.
```

## What the cold does to the ball

An outdoor ball is hard plastic, and plastic stiffens as it cools. A cold ball comes off the paddle faster, bounces lower, and skids, so shots you would normally reach standing up arrive at your shins. Bend your knees a little more than usual and set up half a step closer.

Cold balls also crack, sometimes several in a morning. Bring four or five instead of three, and keep the spares in a jacket pocket rather than on the frozen bench, since a ball that starts warm lasts longer. If one starts to sound flat or dull, look it over. A cracked ball flies crooked, and you will blame yourself for shots that were never your fault.

## What to wear for pickleball in cold weather

Thin layers beat one thick one, because you will be cold for the first ten minutes and warm for the next ninety. I wear a long-sleeved base layer that is not cotton, a quarter-zip over it, and a light vest or windbreaker on top that comes off after the first game. Cotton holds sweat and turns clammy the moment you stop moving, which is why the first layer matters most.

Leggings or lined pants are fine as long as you can lunge in them. A headband or a thin hat keeps your ears from aching. And keep your court shoes on, because a warm, soft-soled sneaker grips a cold court worse than the shoe you already play in.

## Hands are the hard part

Cold fingers hold a paddle too tightly, and a tight grip is tiring and clumsy at the net. Some people play in thin gloves. I have never managed to like the feel, so I keep a hand warmer in each pocket and hold on to them between points. A dry overgrip helps as well, since a handle that was sweaty yesterday is slick and cold today.

## Warm up longer, and check the court

On a cold morning your muscles need more than the usual five minutes, so walk an extra lap and do the heel raises twice. The [warm-up I use](/pickleball-warm-up/) works at any temperature, and in December I simply do more of it.

Then look at the surface before anybody serves. Frost, dew and wet paint are slippery in a way you cannot see from the gate, and a painted line is the slickest part of the court. If your shoe slides when you test it, wait for the sun or go and get coffee. I have done both, and I have never regretted the coffee.

Drink water even though you do not feel thirsty. Cold air is dry, and you are working harder than it feels.

I have only played in the kind of cold where you can still see the court. Below freezing, or in wind that moves the ball around, the people who play indoors have the right idea.
