The first fall I ever saw on a pickleball court took about one second. A lob went up, a woman on the next court took three quick steps straight backward with her eyes on the ball, and her heels got ahead of the rest of her. She sat up, laughed, and waved everyone off. All of us on the surrounding courts quietly learned the same thing that morning. If you want to avoid falling in pickleball, the first rule is the one in the title.

```quick
**The short version:** never run backwards. When a lob goes over your head, turn sideways and go, or let it go. Wear court shoes, not running shoes. Stop the moment a ball rolls onto your court. And leave one game before you are tired, because tired feet are the ones that trip.
```

## Why backwards is the problem

Falls are the big one in this game. In one [analysis of emergency room records](https://www.valuepenguin.com/pickleball-injuries-study), falls were behind roughly four of every ten emergency room visits for pickleball, and more than eight in ten of the people seen were fifty or older. When you backpedal, your weight is on your heels, you cannot see where you are stepping, and there is nothing behind you to catch with. A wrist goes out to break the fall, and wrists are not built for that.

## What to do with a lob instead

Turn first. Drop one foot back so that you are side-on to the net, the way you would stand to catch a ball over your shoulder, and then shuffle or walk back with your feet under you. If the lob is deep, turn all the way and jog to where it will land, as if you were crossing a room.

Better still, let your partner have it. The player diagonal from the lob usually has the easier angle, since they can move sideways and see it the whole way. Say "yours" out loud, early. We settle it before the first serve now: lobs over my head are hers, and lobs over hers are mine.

And some lobs you simply let go. It took me a season to say "nice shot" and mean it. No point at the park is worth a wrist.

## Three smaller ways to avoid falling in pickleball

**Shoes.** A running shoe is tall and soft and built to go forward, and it rolls when you push off sideways. A court shoe is flat and firm at the edges. If you change one thing this year, change that, and the guides for [women](/best-pickleball-shoes-for-women/) and [men](/best-pickleball-shoes-for-men/) have names.

**The court itself.** A ball underfoot is the other classic. When one rolls on, anybody who sees it calls "ball on!" and everyone stops, even mid-rally. Check the surface before you start, too. Morning dew on a painted line is as slick as it looks, and wet leaves in a corner are worse.

**The clock.** Watch when people stumble and it is nearly always late in a session, on a ball they would have reached easily an hour earlier. I leave one game before I think I need to. It felt like quitting for a while, and now it feels like being able to play tomorrow.

## What I have not tried

There are balance classes and strength programs made for exactly this, and people at my courts swear by them. I have not done one, so I will not pretend to review them. The one thing I do is stand on one leg for ten seconds a side before I play, which is part of the [five-minute warm-up](/pickleball-warm-up/). If you have already had a fall, or you feel unsteady off the court as well, that is worth a conversation with your doctor before your next game.

Turn, say "yours," wear the right shoes, and go home while your legs still feel good. I would like to see all of us out there for a long time.
