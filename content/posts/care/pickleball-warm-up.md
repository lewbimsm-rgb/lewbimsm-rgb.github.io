For my first year, my pickleball warm-up was the walk from the car. I would drop my bag, pick up a paddle, and be lunging for a dink ninety seconds later on legs that had been sitting in traffic. Nothing terrible happened. My calves simply filed a complaint every single evening, and it took me far too long to connect the two.

```quick
**The short version:** five minutes, standing up, before the first serve. Walk two brisk laps of the court. Do ten heel raises at the fence. Shuffle sideways along the baseline and back. Circle your arms and turn your shoulders. Stand on one leg for ten seconds, then the other. Finish with a minute of soft dinks.
```

## Why a pickleball warm-up matters more at our age

Pickleball looks gentle, and then it asks you to start, stop and change direction a few hundred times in a morning. Cold calves and a stiff Achilles do not like sudden starts, which is why so many of the injuries people talk about at the courts happen in the first game rather than the last. Five minutes of easy movement gets blood into the legs before you ask them for anything quick.

I built mine from a list the [Hospital for Special Surgery](https://www.hss.edu/health-library/move-better/stretches-exercises-prevent-pickleball-injuries) publishes for pickleball players, which includes heel raises, leg lifts and single-leg balance. I kept the ones I could do standing at a fence in front of people, because a warm-up you are embarrassed to do is a warm-up you skip.

## The five minutes, in order

**Walk two laps, briskly.** Around the outside of the court, arms swinging, a little faster than is comfortable for talking. It takes about two minutes, and it is the part that counts most.

**Ten heel raises.** Hold the fence, rise onto your toes, and come down slowly. The hospital's list has these for ankle strength, and the slow way down is the half I used to rush.

**Side steps along the baseline.** Shuffle from one sideline to the other and back, knees soft, feet never crossing. This is the movement the game is made of, and it wakes up the hips, which do more of the work than most of us realize.

**Arm circles and a shoulder turn.** Ten small circles forward, ten back, then turn your shoulders left and right with the paddle held across your chest. An overhead on a cold shoulder is a memorable mistake.

**One leg at a time.** Stand on one foot for a slow count of ten, then switch, with a hand near the fence if you want it. I wobble every time. Balance is the thing that keeps you upright when you reach for a wide ball, and it improves with exactly this much practice.

**A minute of soft dinks.** Now pick up the paddle. Stand at the kitchen line with whoever is nearby and tap the ball back and forth, softly, until your hands feel like yours.

## What I do not do

I do not sit down on the court for a long stretching session before the first game. The doctors at that hospital do say to stretch and warm up before you play, so after the laps I do a gentle hamstring stretch standing at the fence, and I save the long, still stretches for the living room floor afterward. I am also not a physical therapist. If something hurts in a sharp or new way, that is a question for one of them, and no routine from a blog should talk you out of asking.

## When there is no time

Some mornings the game is starting and four people are waiting on you. Do the laps anyway, even one, and tell your partner you will take the first two points gently. Nobody has ever minded.
