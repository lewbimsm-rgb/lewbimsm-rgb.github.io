## Why a 9 a.m. doubles game is a sun problem

A morning session feels safe because it is cool. It is not. UV strengthens through the morning, and the American Academy of Dermatology names 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. as the hours when the sun's rays are strongest. A 9-to-11 session spends half its time inside that window. Hard courts also bounce some light back up at you. A visor shades your eyes and leaves your chin, neck and ears to the court.

Then there is the exposed skin. Doubles leaves your forearms, the backs of your hands, your neck and the tops of your ears out for two hours. Many players do that three times a week. One in five Americans develops skin cancer by age 70. That figure comes from the [Skin Cancer Foundation](https://www.skincancer.org/skin-cancer-information/skin-cancer-facts/). The same page links most skin cancers to sun exposure.

## The only pickleball skincare routine that matters

The whole routine fits on an index card. Choose a sunscreen that says broad spectrum (it blocks UVA and UVB), SPF 30 or higher, and water resistant. That is the [American Academy of Dermatology's](https://www.aad.org/media/stats-sunscreen) standing advice. Put it on 15 minutes before you go out. Use enough: about one ounce, a shot glass, covers an adult body, and most people use far less. Cover the parts players forget: ears, the back of the neck and the backs of the hands. Add the part in your hair and the tops of your feet if you wear low socks.

Reapply at least every two hours. Playing past two hours? Put on a fresh coat at the two-hour mark, and sooner if you have sweated hard or toweled off. Keep a travel tube in the paddle bag so there is no excuse.

Lips burn too. The AAD recommends a lip balm with SPF; we carry an SPF 30. Then add the clothing layer. Start with a hat that has a brim all the way around, since a cap or visor leaves ears and neck out. Add sunglasses and UPF 50 sun sleeves for your forearms. Sleeves feel cooler than bare skin in direct sun, and they save you from reapplying on your arms.

## Mineral or chemical: pick the one you will wear

Mineral sunscreens use zinc oxide or titanium dioxide. They sit on top of the skin, work the moment you put them on, and rarely sting. That makes them the usual choice for sensitive skin, rosacea, and eyes that burn when you sweat. The trade-off is texture. They spread thicker and can leave a white or gray cast, more so on deeper skin tones.

Chemical sunscreens use filters like avobenzone and octocrylene. They rub in clear, feel lighter under sweat, and go on faster over a hairy forearm. They need about 15 minutes to start working, and some players find they sting the eyes. The AAD's position is that both types work when you use enough and reapply. The best one is the one you will wear every session. Hybrids exist too: EltaMD UV Clear pairs zinc oxide with a chemical filter to get the lighter feel.

One note on labels. "Water resistant" on a U.S. sunscreen means it held its SPF through 40 or 80 minutes of water or sweat in testing. Nothing is sweatproof. A two-hour session needs a reapply either way.

## Skin over 50: what changes and what to watch

Skin thins and dries with age. It heals slower, and it carries every summer you spent without sunscreen decades ago. That is why new spots show up now. Good pickleball skincare after 50 is mostly subtraction: fewer products, more moisturizer, and a hat you actually wear. If your dermatologist has you on a prescription retinoid, it can make skin more sensitive to sun. The hat and the reapply matter even more then.

The one addition is the yearly check. The [Skin Cancer Foundation](https://www.skincancer.org/skin-cancer-information/skin-cancer-facts/) advises a monthly self-check. Add a professional skin exam once a year. Learn the ABCDEs (asymmetry, border, color, diameter, evolving). Book an appointment for any spot that is new, changing or bleeding. Same for a sore that does not heal in a few weeks. Found early, melanoma is highly treatable. That appointment matters more than any product on this page.

## What to skip

Most pickleball skincare mistakes are habits, not products. Here are the ones we see at the courts.

**SPF in your foundation or moisturizer as your only protection.** You rarely put on enough of either to reach the SPF on the label. Use a real sunscreen underneath.

**Spray sunscreen on a windy court.** Half of it lands on the bench. If you use a spray, spray it into your hands and rub it on, and never spray your face.

**Last summer's tube from the hot car.** Sunscreen has an expiration date. A tube cooked in a car all summer is past its best regardless. Buy fresh each season.

**SPF 100 as an excuse.** A higher number adds a little protection, not a license to skip the reapply.

**A base tan or tanning oil.** There is no safe tan. The AAD is blunt on this: a tan is skin damage.

**The ten-step routine.** Cleanser, moisturizer, sunscreen. That is the routine.

## Frequently asked questions

```faq
Q: What SPF do I need for pickleball?
A: SPF 30 or higher, broad spectrum, and water resistant, per the American Academy of Dermatology. Higher numbers add a little, not a lot. Using a full amount and reapplying every two hours matters more than the number on the bottle.

Q: Do I need to reapply sunscreen during a two-hour session?
A: Yes, if you keep playing. The AAD says to reapply at least every two hours, and sooner after heavy sweating or toweling off. For a two-hour session, put on a fresh coat at the two-hour mark if you stay on. Do it earlier on a hot, sweaty morning.

Q: Is mineral or chemical sunscreen better for pickleball?
A: Both work when you use enough. Mineral (zinc, titanium) suits sensitive skin and eyes that sting when you sweat. Chemical rubs in clear and feels lighter. Pick the one you will put on every session.

Q: Does pickleball skincare need a toner or a serum?
A: No. Sunscreen before play, a rinse and a plain moisturizer after, and a yearly skin check cover it. A toner is optional; if you like one, pick an alcohol-free version. Serums and ten-step routines add cost and irritation without adding sun protection.

Q: How often should a pickleball player see a dermatologist?
A: Once a year for a full skin exam, plus a monthly self-check at home. That is the Skin Cancer Foundation's guidance. Go sooner for any spot that is new, changing, bleeding, or not healing in a few weeks.
```
