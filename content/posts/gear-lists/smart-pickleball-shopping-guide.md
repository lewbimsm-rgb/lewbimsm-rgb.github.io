Court shoes and sun protection are where the money goes, a control paddle for $60 to $120 comes next, and the pro gear stays on the shelf for now. That is this whole pickleball shopping guide in one sentence, and the rest of the page is the why.

A rolled ankle costs you a month of play and a bad sunburn costs you a week, and a flat-soled court shoe plus a tube of sunscreen heads off both for less than a pro paddle costs. The paddle comes third because a $100 control paddle and a $280 pro paddle hit the same dink, and the pro one adds power and spin that most of us cannot use yet.

```quick
**The short answer:** court shoes first, sunscreen and a hat second, then a 16 mm control paddle for $60 to $120. Balls and an overgrip cost so little that you may as well buy the right ones. A bag, a nicer bottle and better clothes can wait a month, and the $250 paddles, braces and gadgets can wait until you know why you want them.
```

## The pickleball shopping guide, category by category

### Shoes, the one place not to save

Running shoes have a tall, soft heel built for straight lines, and pickleball is almost all sideways, so that heel rolls when you push toward the sideline, which is how ankles turn in week one. A court shoe has a flat, grippy sole and support along the outside edge, and for $70 to $130 it takes that injury off the table.

The ASICS Gel-Rocket line is the cheapest real court shoe from a big brand, the Gel-Resolution is the cushioned step up, and New Balance makes wide widths. Try them on late in the day in the socks you play in, because feet swell; the [women's picks](/best-pickleball-shoes-for-women/) and [men's picks](/best-pickleball-shoes-for-men/) go deeper on fit.

```buyif
+ you play on outdoor courts, where the surface grabs and twists
+ your knees or ankles complain after a session in running shoes
- you already own a tennis or volleyball shoe (that works)
- you play only indoors on a wood floor (any flat-soled court shoe will do)
```

### A paddle, at $60 to $120

A 16 mm control paddle with a 4.25" grip is plenty for the first year, because it keeps the ball in and is easy on the elbow. The Selkirk SLK Halo (Control) is my pick, and the entry paddles from JOOLA and Selkirk do the job under $100. The $250 pro paddles reward a fast, clean swing and punish a late one, so wait until year two and see if your game asks for one. The [Selkirk vs JOOLA vs CRBN comparison](/selkirk-vs-joola-vs-crbn-paddles/) has the specs side by side.

### Sun protection counts as gear

The [American Academy of Dermatology](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/sun-protection/sunscreen-patient/sunscreen-faqs) says SPF 30 or higher, broad spectrum and water resistant, reapplied every two hours, and a two-hour open play session is exactly one reapply. Skin at our age is thinner and carries more sun history, so add a hat with a brim, sunglasses and sun sleeves. I have forgotten mine more than once, and my nose reminded me for a week. The [skincare guide](/pickleball-skincare/) covers mineral versus chemical sunscreen.

### The right balls

Outdoor balls are harder, with 40 small holes, so they fly straight in wind, and indoor balls are softer, with 26 larger holes. Ask what your group plays and buy that; most parks use an outdoor ball like the Franklin X-40. Balls crack in the cold, which is why a six-pack beats a three-pack.

### A water bottle, bought once

A two-hour session on a hot court needs more water than a small plastic bottle holds, so insulated steel in the 24 to 40 oz range is worth buying once. The Owala FreeSip is my pick for a court bag, and the [water bottle guide](/best-water-bottles-for-pickleball/) covers YETI, Stanley and Hydro Flask.

### Everything else can wait

Any wicking tee and shorts you already own will do, as long as they are not cotton, because a wet cotton shirt gets heavy and cold. A tote or an old backpack carries everything until you know you will keep playing, and when you are ready for a real bag, the [bag checklist](/what-to-pack-in-your-pickleball-bag/) lists what belongs in it. Of the small things, an overgrip is worth it, because it soaks up sweat for the price of a coffee, and lead tape is not, because it adds weight to the paddle head. Braces help when a physical therapist or doctor says they will, and not before.

## What it adds up to

The kit most readers settle into is about $300: the Selkirk SLK Halo (Control) at $100 to $130, court shoes with real cushion at about $90, a six-pack of balls and two overgrips for about $25, EltaMD UV Clear and a brimmed hat for about $55, and the Owala FreeSip 32 oz for about $30. Nothing in it needs replacing for a year except the balls and the overgrip.

I have not bought everything on this page; the picks come from the brands' spec pages, the fit ranges I use across the site, and what the players I know tell me after a season. ## Common questions

```faq
Q: How much does it cost to start playing pickleball?
A: Under $150 if you already own a wicking shirt, a cap and a water bottle. An entry paddle, court shoes on sale and a few outdoor balls cover the first month, and many parks and rec centers lend paddles for open play, so you can try before you buy.

Q: Is an expensive paddle worth it for a beginner?
A: For a beginner, no. A $60 to $120 paddle with a 16 mm core and a 4.25" grip hits every shot you need in year one. The $250 pro paddles add power and spin that take a year to use, and weight that an elbow does not need, so spend the difference on shoes.

Q: What should I buy first, a paddle or shoes?
A: Shoes, every time. A borrowed paddle gets you through the first few sessions, but court shoes need to be on your feet from day one, because that is the week ankles roll. Once you know you will keep playing, add a 16 mm control paddle in the $60 to $120 range and a good sunscreen.
```
