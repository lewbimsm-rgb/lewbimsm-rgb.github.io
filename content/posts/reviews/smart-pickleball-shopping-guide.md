## Where your money matters

Rec players lose more to injuries than to bad gear. A rolled ankle costs you a month of play, and a bad sunburn costs you a week. The two cheapest things here, court shoes and sunscreen, prevent both. That is why they sit at the top of this pickleball shopping guide.

The paddle comes third, not first. A $100 control paddle and a $280 pro paddle hit the same dink. The pro paddle adds power and spin that most rec players cannot use yet. It also adds weight that a 50-plus elbow does not want.

After that, the list gets cheap and quick. Balls, an overgrip and a bottle you already own cover the first month. Clothing, a bag and accessories can follow once you know you will keep playing.

## The pickleball shopping guide, category by category

Each category below gets a verdict and a budget band. The verdicts are worth it, not yet or skip. The bands are rough, because prices move every week.

### Paddle: worth it at $60 to $120

**Verdict: worth it, at the right price.** A 16 mm control paddle with a 4.25" grip, under 8.0 oz, is plenty for year one. The Selkirk SLK Halo (Control) is our pick, and the entry paddles from JOOLA and Selkirk work under $100. Our [Selkirk vs JOOLA vs CRBN comparison](/selkirk-vs-joola-vs-crbn-paddles/) walks through the specs.

**Skip for now:** the $250 pro paddles. They reward a fast, clean swing and punish a late one. Buy one in year two if your game asks for it.

```buyif
+ you want a paddle that keeps the ball in and is easy on the elbow
+ you plan to play at least once a week for a season
- you already own a paddle that feels fine (play it until it goes soft)
- you want the cheapest option (a $50 entry paddle is fine for month one)
```

### Shoes: the one place not to save

**Verdict: worth it, spend here first.** Running shoes have a tall, soft heel built for straight lines. Pickleball is sideways. Side-to-side pushes on a tall heel are how ankles roll and knees twist. A court shoe has a flat, grippy sole and support on the outside edge. That is the injury you prevent for about $70 to $130.

The ASICS Gel-Rocket line is the cheapest real court shoe from a big brand. The Gel-Resolution line is the cushioned step up. New Balance offers wide widths, and On and Nike cover the lighter, faster end. Our [women's shoe picks](/best-pickleball-shoes-for-women/) and [men's shoe picks](/best-pickleball-shoes-for-men/) go deeper.

Try shoes on late in the day with the socks you play in. Replace them when the sole goes smooth or the heel leans.

```buyif
+ you play on outdoor courts, where the surface grabs and twists
+ your knees or ankles complain after a session in running shoes
- you already own a tennis or volleyball shoe (that works)
- you play only indoors on a wood floor (any flat-soled court shoe will do)
```

### Balls: cheap, so buy the right type

**Verdict: worth it, and cheap.** Outdoor balls are harder, with 40 small holes, so they fly straight in wind. Indoor balls are softer, with 26 larger holes. Most parks use an outdoor ball like the Franklin X-40, and most rec centers use an indoor one. Ask what your group plays and buy that. Balls crack in the cold, so a six-pack beats a three-pack. For tournaments, [USA Pickleball](https://usapickleball.org/) keeps a list of approved balls.

### Clothing: wear what you own first

**Verdict: not yet.** Any wicking tee and shorts you already own will do for the first month. Cotton is the one thing to avoid, because a wet cotton shirt gets heavy and cold. When you are ready, buy one good skort or one pair of lined shorts with a pocket for a ball. See the [women's outfit guide](/cute-pickleball-outfits-for-women/) and [men's outfit guide](/pickleball-outfits-for-men/).

### Bag: nice, not urgent

**Verdict: not yet.** A tote or an old backpack works until you know you will keep playing. When you buy one, look for a sling with a fence hook, a shoe pocket and room for a bottle. Our [bag checklist](/what-to-pack-in-your-pickleball-bag/) lists the 15 things that belong in it.

### Sun protection: non-negotiable

**Verdict: worth it, the cheapest insurance you will buy.** The [American Academy of Dermatology](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/sun-protection/sunscreen-patient/sunscreen-faqs) says SPF 30 or higher, broad spectrum, water resistant. Reapply every two hours. A two-hour open play session is exactly one reapply. Add a hat with a brim, sunglasses and sun sleeves. Your arms and neck stop paying for every morning on court.

Skin over 50 is thinner and carries more sun history, so this matters more, not less. Our [pickleball skincare guide](/pickleball-skincare/) covers mineral versus chemical sunscreen and what to do after you play.

```buyif
+ you play outdoors at any time of day
+ you have fair skin, a history of sunburn or a dermatologist who nags you
- you play only indoors (a daily SPF moisturizer still helps)
- you already have a sunscreen you like and wear (keep using it)
```

### Accessories: overgrip yes, lead tape no, braces only with a reason

**Overgrip: yes.** It costs about as much as a coffee, soaks up sweat and adds a little cushion for sore hands. Replace it when it goes shiny.

**Lead tape: no.** It adds weight to the paddle head. More weight is the opposite of what an elbow over 50 wants.

**Braces and sleeves: only with a reason.** A knee sleeve or elbow strap helps when a physical therapist or doctor says it will. Without that, a brace hides a problem instead of fixing it.

**A ball pick-up tube: yes, if bending hurts.** Our [accessories list](/pickleball-accessories/) covers ten small things that fix big annoyances.

## Mistakes people make

**Buying the paddle first.** The paddle is the fun purchase, so it happens first. The shoes are the purchase that keeps you on the court.

**Playing in running shoes "for now."** Rolled ankles tend to happen in week one, before anyone has bought court shoes.

**Buying a pro paddle because a pro uses it.** Pros swing faster than we do. A control paddle at a third of the price fits a rec game better.

**Skipping sunscreen because the session is short.** Two hours on a reflective court at nine in the morning is a lot of sun, and it adds up over a season.

## Common questions

```faq
Q: How much does it cost to start playing pickleball?
A: Under $150 if you already own a wicking shirt, a cap and a water bottle. An entry paddle, court shoes on sale and a few outdoor balls cover the first month. Many parks and rec centers also lend paddles for open play, so you can try before you buy.

Q: Is an expensive paddle worth it for a beginner?
A: No. A $60 to $120 paddle with a 16 mm core and a 4.25" grip hits every shot a beginner needs. The $250 pro paddles add power and spin that take a year to use, and weight that an elbow does not need. Spend the difference on shoes.

Q: Can I play pickleball in running shoes?
A: You can, but it is the most common way new players get hurt. Running shoes have a tall, soft heel for forward motion, and pickleball moves sideways. A court shoe with a flat sole and outside-edge support costs about the same and prevents rolled ankles.

Q: What should I buy first, a paddle or shoes?
A: Shoes. Borrow a paddle for the first few sessions if you must, but wear court shoes from day one. Once you know you will keep playing, buy a 16 mm control paddle in the $60 to $120 range and a good sunscreen.

Q: Do I need a pickleball bag?
A: Not yet. A tote or an old backpack carries a paddle, balls, a bottle and sunscreen just fine. Buy a sling bag with a fence hook and a shoe pocket once you play twice a week and want your keys where you can find them.
```
