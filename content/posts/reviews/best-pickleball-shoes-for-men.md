## Why running shoes roll ankles

A running shoe moves you forward. Its sole is tall, soft and curved, and its upper is thin mesh. Pickleball is a sideways game: a shuffle, a lunge, a hard stop at the kitchen line. When you push off sideways in a running shoe, the tall sole tips. The mesh then lets your foot slide over the edge. That is the rolled ankle that sends men home from open play.

A court shoe stops that with a flat sole, a firm wall along the outside of the foot and a wider base under the heel. The [American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons](https://www.orthoinfo.org/staying-healthy/athletic-shoes/) says it plainly: court shoes provide stability in all directions. Tennis shoes, volleyball shoes (indoors) and the new pickleball-specific models all qualify. Running, walking and trail shoes do not.

## What the best pickleball shoes for men need

**Side-to-side (lateral) support.** Press the side of the shoe with your thumb. You want a firm wall, not soft mesh. Many court shoes add a lip of rubber outside the midsole, called an outrigger. It widens the base when you lunge.

**A flat, grippy outsole.** A zigzag (herringbone) pattern that sits flat on the ground. Hard rubber for outdoor concrete; softer gum rubber for an indoor wood floor.

**Cushion under the heel.** Your knees take the stops. Look for a gel pad or a thick foam layer under the heel and firmer foam under the arch.

**Width options.** Men's shoes come in D (standard), 2E (wide) and sometimes 4E (extra wide). If your little toe rubs or your forefoot spreads, a 2E fixes it. New Balance sells the most widths.

**Weight.** Light feels fast, but the lightest shoes give up the side wall. A mid-weight shoe is the safer buy for most men over 50.

**A toe cap.** If you drag your back toe on serves, a rubber wrap over the toe stops the upper from wearing through.

## Three kinds of court shoe

**Performance** shoes are firm, stable and grippy, built for men who move fast and stop hard. They feel stiff on day one and last the longest.

**Comfort** shoes carry more cushion, run wider and go easier on knees and hips. They give up a little speed and a little outsole life.

**Value** shoes cost under about $80 and still have a real court sole. You give up some durability and most width options, not safety.

## Heavier players and bad knees

If you are over about 200 pounds, soft foam is not your friend. It packs down under your weight within weeks, the shoe starts to lean, and your ankle follows. Pick a firm midsole and a wide base: the adidas Barricade, the ASICS Gel-Resolution or the New Balance 796 in a 2E. Firm feels harsh for a week and then feels normal.

Bad knees want two things that pull in opposite directions: cushion under the heel and a shoe that does not wobble. The New Balance FuelCell 996 and the Skechers Viper Court are the softest shoes here that still have a side wall. Add a thin cushioned insole if you need more, and keep an instant ice pack in your bag for the drive home. If a knee hurts before you play, not just after, see a physical therapist. No shoe fixes that.

## When to replace your shoes

Court shoes die from the bottom up. Look at three things once a month. First, the outsole: when the zigzag tread under the ball of your foot goes smooth, you have lost your grip. Second, the heel: set the shoes on a table and look from behind; if one leans inward, the foam has collapsed. Third, the side wall: if your foot now slides toward the edge on a lunge, the wall has softened.

As a rule of thumb, a pair lasts roughly 60 to 80 hours on outdoor courts. If you play two hours, three times a week, that is about three months. Indoor players get much longer. If you can, rotate two pairs and let each one dry out between sessions.

## Mistakes people make

**Playing in running or walking shoes.** The tall, soft sole is the cause of most rolled ankles at open play.

**Buying by size alone.** A 10 in ASICS, New Balance and Nike are three different shapes. Try them on late in the day, in your court socks, or order two sizes and send one back.

**Keeping shoes until they look worn.** The upper outlasts the outsole and the foam. Judge the shoe by the tread and the heel, not the laces.

## How we chose the best pickleball shoes for men

We did not run a lab test or weigh these shoes. The picks rest on the makers' published specs and width charts, and on what players report about fit and durability. We also watch what works on the feet of men at our local courts. The family will add on-court notes and photos as they wear them.

```faq
Q: Can I play pickleball in running shoes?
A: You can, but not for long. Running shoes have a tall, soft, curved sole built for forward motion. Pickleball is mostly side-to-side, and that sole tips when you push sideways. A court shoe has a flatter sole and a firm side wall that holds your foot in place.

Q: What are the best pickleball shoes for men with wide feet?
A: Start with New Balance, which sells the 796 in D, 2E and some 4E widths and the FuelCell 996 in wide. Some ASICS Gel-Resolution versions also come in wide. Skechers runs roomy. Nike and adidas tend to run narrow, so try them on first.

Q: Are pickleball shoes different from tennis shoes?
A: Not by much. Both have a flat outsole, a firm side wall and cushion for hard stops. Pickleball-specific shoes from Skechers, K-Swiss and HEAD tune the fit and grip for the smaller court. A good tennis shoe from ASICS, New Balance or adidas works just as well.

Q: How often should I replace pickleball shoes?
A: Judge the shoe, not the calendar. Replace it when the tread under the ball of your foot goes smooth, when the heel leans on a flat table, or when your foot slides to the edge on a lunge. As a rough guide, outdoor players get 60 to 80 hours per pair.
```
